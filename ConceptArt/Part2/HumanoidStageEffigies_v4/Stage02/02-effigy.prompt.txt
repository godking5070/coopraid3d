LATEST USER CORRECTION — HUMAN BODY SHAPE IS ESSENTIAL. The user liked the humanlike proportions of a ceremonial effigy, NOT bosses whose entire body becomes a furnace, cup, building, barrel or tombstone. Design a clearly HUMAN-SHAPED ritual statue: about 7.5 heads tall, attached head and neck, two shoulders, recognizable human ribcage, narrower WAIST, distinct PELVIS, exactly TWO upper-arm/forearm/hand chains and TWO long thigh/shin/ankle/foot chains. Long legs about half body height. Even crouching, keep the waist, hips, thighs, knees and ankles recognizable. Every device is integrated into or mounted along a human-proportioned torso/back/limb, NEVER replaces the whole torso with a giant object. No short plinth legs, no barrel abdomen, globe body or architectural arch chest.
Retain each stage's own story and external shell identity. Do not use the same diamond cap head, V breastplate and pointed hip fins for everyone. Different head enclosures, chest shells, limb surfaces and stage-specific mechanisms; shared human proportions are intentional. It remains an uncanny inanimate EFFIGY, not an attractive playable adventurer. No human face, eyes/pupils, nose/lips, expression, fashion hair or bald human scalp. Blank masks, closed visors or fixed ceremonial planes, no knight portrait.
Reference images are Part I art direction ONLY: controlled delicate outlines, large ivory/soft-gold/navy fixed shell surfaces, softly shaded 2-3 tone lavender CEL illustration, modelable joints and negative spaces. No exact face/hair/outfit/source weapons or sheet layout. No PBR, photo skin, flesh wrinkles, dirt, rust or hundreds of ornamental gears. Simple sculptural construction.
ONE full-body subject at 1536x1024 landscape, quiet three-quarter perspective and readable contact points. Whole head, hands/fingers, feet, large mechanisms, sphere/rings and weapons in frame with at least 5% margin. Do not crop any main part. Low-contrast stage-appropriate background. No panels, text, labels, UI, additional figures, T pose or turnaround sheet.
